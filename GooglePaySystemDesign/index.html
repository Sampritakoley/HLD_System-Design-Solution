01 / Understanding the problem

What is this system, really?

A payments app lets users send money to anyone with a phone number, UPI id or QR code, pay merchants, and request money, in seconds, directly between bank accounts. The app itself does not hold most of the money: banks do, and a national switch (NPCI in India) connects them.

The core tension: this is a distributed system where every external call can time out, yet the answer to “did the money move?” must always be exactly right. Correctness beats speed; speed still matters a lot.

Core features in scope

Link account

Register the user, verify the phone, link bank accounts, set a UPI PIN with the bank.

Pay & collect

Send to a contact, UPI id or QR; request money; pay merchants and bills.

History & status

Transaction list, receipts, status tracking, disputes and refunds.

Rewards & wallet

Cashback, offers and an in-app balance recorded in our own ledger.

What makes this hard at scale

  • Exactly-once money movement. Retries and timeouts must never double-debit or lose a payment.
  • Unreliable partners. Banks and the switch are slow, rate-limited and sometimes down.
  • Unknown outcomes. A timeout does not tell us whether the debit happened.
  • Fraud and abuse. Decisions are needed in under 100 ms, inline with the payment.
  • Peak load. Festivals and sales multiply traffic 5-10× in minutes.
02 / Requirements

Functional vs. non-functional requirements

Non-functional requirements drive the architecture, so they come first.

Functional

  • Onboard and link bank accounts
  • Send money by phone, UPI id, QR
  • Collect requests and approvals
  • Check balance and see history
  • Payment status, receipts, disputes
  • Merchant payments and bill pay
  • Refunds and reversals
  • Notifications for every event

Non-functional

  • 50M DAU, ~100M txns/day, ~10K TPS peak
  • Payment completes in < 3 s (p95)
  • 99.99% availability for payments
  • Strong consistency and exactly-once effect
  • No lost or duplicated money, ever
  • Fraud decision < 100 ms inline
  • Full audit trail, encryption, compliance
  • Degrade gracefully when a bank is down

Clarifying questions

Do we hold money (wallet) or only move it between bank accounts via UPI?
Which parts are ours versus the bank and switch: PIN entry, debit, credit, settlement?
What is the expected behavior when the outcome is unknown after a timeout?
Do we need international payments, multiple currencies, or cards?
What are the fraud, regulatory and data residency requirements?

Storage

Transactions: 100M/day × 1 KB = 100 GB/day ≈ ~36 TB/year
Ledger entries: 200M/day × ~200 B = ~40 GB/day ≈ ~15 TB/year
User and account data: 300M users × ~2 KB = ~600 GB
Audit and events: stream to Kafka → data lake, retained for years (compliance)

Hot data is recent transactions; archive old ones to cheaper storage
but keep them queryable for disputes.

Latency budget

Total target ~3 s: app + our services ~300 ms, fraud ~100 ms,
switch + issuing bank + beneficiary bank ~1-2 s.
Most latency is outside our control, so we make our part fast and
handle the slow or unknown cases explicitly.

 │                    ▲ callbacks / webhooks

 └─► [ Kafka ] ─► Notifications · Reconciliation · Analytics · Audit

Payments are a state machine persisted before every external call

Component rationale

API gateway
Authenticates the device and user, rate limits, and rejects duplicate requests by idempotency key before they reach core services.
Payment service
Owns the transaction state machine. Persists state before and after each external step so any crash can resume or compensate.
Risk / fraud service
Scores each payment in real time with rules and ML, and can allow, challenge or block, within a strict latency budget.
Ledger service
Immutable double-entry record of every money movement in our books (wallet, rewards, merchant settlement). The source of financial truth.
PSP / bank adapter
Speaks the UPI protocol to the switch and banks, with timeouts, retries, circuit breakers and per-bank rate limits.
Status service
Shows live status to users and merchants, via push and cached polling, so the payment path is not hit by status checks.
Kafka
Streams events to notifications, reconciliation, analytics and audit without slowing the payment path.

End-to-end flow — "Aisha pays Ben ₹500"

  1. Aisha enters the amount and Ben’s UPI id; the app sends the request with a unique idempotency key.
  2. Payment Service creates the transaction as INITIATED in the DB and calls Risk, which returns allow, step-up or block.
  3. On allow, state becomes PENDING; Aisha enters her UPI PIN, which goes securely to her bank and not through our servers.
  4. The adapter sends the pay request to the switch; the switch asks Aisha’s bank to debit and Ben’s bank to credit.
  5. Callbacks report success or failure; the state moves to SUCCESS or FAILED with a conditional update.
  6. Any wallet or reward effect is recorded in the Ledger as balanced debit and credit entries.
  7. If no callback arrives, the state is UNKNOWN: a status-enquiry job asks the switch until a final answer is known.
  8. Kafka events send notifications to both users and feed reconciliation with bank settlement files.

Different data, different access patterns, different stores. Money data lives in ACID databases.

Ledger entriesPostgreSQL / Spanner (append-only)Immutable double-entry, strong consistency

Core tables and keys

-- Transactions
CREATE TABLE transactions (
  txn_id        UUID PRIMARY KEY,
  idem_key      TEXT UNIQUE NOT NULL,   -- one effect per client request
  payer_vpa     TEXT, payee_vpa TEXT,
  amount_paise  BIGINT NOT NULL CHECK (amount_paise > 0),
  status        TEXT NOT NULL,  -- INITIATED | PENDING | SUCCESS | FAILED | UNKNOWN | REVERSED
  switch_ref    TEXT,           -- reference from the UPI switch
  created_at    TIMESTAMP DEFAULT now(), updated_at TIMESTAMP
);

-- Double-entry ledger (append-only, never updated)
CREATE TABLE ledger_entries (
  entry_id    UUID PRIMARY KEY,
  txn_id      UUID NOT NULL,
  account_id  BIGINT NOT NULL,
  direction   CHAR(1) NOT NULL,  -- D or C
  amount_paise BIGINT NOT NULL,
  created_at  TIMESTAMP DEFAULT now()
);  -- invariant: SUM(debits) = SUM(credits) per txn_id

Sharding strategy

  • Transactions — shard by payer user_id so a user’s history is one shard; the idempotency key is unique per shard (include user in the key).
  • Ledger — shard by account_id; keep both legs of a transfer in one transaction or tie them with a saga.
  • History — recent in the primary DB, older archived by month, still retrievable for disputes.
  • Replicas — synchronous replication within a region for durability; async cross-region for disaster recovery.
06 / Deep dives

Deep dive on 3 critical components

For each: the problem, the naive solution, why it fails, and the real solution. Volunteer these before the interviewer asks.

01 Transaction processing — exactly-once payments over unreliable banks

The problem: A payment crosses our service, the switch and two banks. Any call may time out, return twice, or arrive late. The result must be exactly one debit and one credit, or none.

Naive solution — call the bank and trust the response
resp = bank.debit(payer, amount)     # timeout? retry?
if resp.ok: bank.credit(payee, amount)

Why it fails: A timeout does not mean failure: the bank may have debited. Retrying then double-charges; not retrying may leave the user debited with nothing delivered. A crash between debit and credit strands money.

Real solution — persisted state machine, idempotency, enquiry and reversal
1. Idempotency key from the client for every payment. Enforced by a
   UNIQUE constraint; a retry returns the existing transaction.

2. Persist before acting: write the txn as INITIATED/PENDING, then call
   out. After a crash, a recovery job resumes from the last state.

3. Pass our txn_id to the switch as the reference, so a repeated
   request is recognized as the same payment, not a new one.

4. Timeouts → UNKNOWN, never assume failure. A status-enquiry job
   polls the switch (with backoff) until a final SUCCESS or FAILED.

5. Conditional state transitions:
     UPDATE transactions SET status='SUCCESS'
     WHERE txn_id=? AND status IN ('PENDING','UNKNOWN')
   Late or duplicate callbacks affect 0 rows and are ignored.

6. Debit succeeded but credit failed → automatic reversal (refund)
   within the time limit set by the scheme; track it as REVERSED.

7. Daily reconciliation: compare our records with the switch and bank
   settlement files; investigate mismatches in a dedicated queue.

The user sees "Processing" for unknown cases. Never show a
failure that might later turn out to be a success.
Treat every external call as “might have happened.” Persist intent first, use idempotent references, and resolve uncertainty by asking, never by guessing.
02 Ledger & consistency — balances that always add up

The problem: Wallet balances, rewards and merchant settlements must be exact. Concurrent payments, retries and failures must not create or destroy money.

Naive solution — a balance column updated in place
bal = SELECT balance FROM wallets WHERE id=?;
UPDATE wallets SET balance = bal - 500 WHERE id=?;   -- read-modify-write

Why it fails: Two concurrent debits both read 800 and write 300, losing a deduction and allowing an overdraft. A bug or crash can change the balance with no record of why, so there is nothing to audit or repair.

Real solution — append-only double-entry ledger with atomic transactions
1. Every movement is two entries: a debit and a credit of equal amount,
   inserted in one DB transaction. The ledger is append-only: fix
   mistakes with a new compensating entry, never an UPDATE or DELETE.

2. Balance = sum of entries (or a maintained balance row updated in the
   same transaction):
     UPDATE accounts SET balance = balance - :amt
     WHERE id=? AND balance >= :amt     -- 0 rows → insufficient funds

3. Idempotent writes: unique key (txn_id, account_id, direction) so a
   replay inserts nothing new.

4. Cross-shard transfers: use a saga (debit leg, then credit leg with
   retries and a compensating reversal) or a transactional database
   (Spanner-style) for atomic cross-shard commits.

5. Hot accounts (large merchants): split into sub-accounts and
   aggregate, or batch credits per second instead of one row update
   per payment.

6. Invariants checked continuously: per-txn debits = credits, and total
   system money = known external balances. Alerts on any drift.

Cache balances for display only. Decisions use the
database, never the cache.
Never store “current state” as the only truth. Store the immutable history of changes; balances are derived, and every number can be explained and audited.
03 Fraud & risk — block bad payments in under 100 ms

The problem: Scams, stolen devices and money-mule rings run through the same rails as honest payments. We must decide in real time without annoying good users.

Naive solution — review transactions afterwards in batch
nightly job: SELECT suspicious patterns → freeze accounts

Why it fails: Money is gone within seconds and cannot be clawed back easily. A daily batch finds fraud long after the loss, while blunt blocking rules reject too many legitimate payments.

Real solution — inline scoring with fast features, plus an async feedback loop
1. Inline Risk call before the debit, with a strict timeout (~100 ms).
   If risk is unavailable, apply a safe default (allow small amounts,
   challenge or block large ones).

2. Layers: hard rules first (blocklists, velocity limits like "5
   payments to new payees in 10 minutes", amount caps), then an ML
   score from features.

3. Features served from a low-latency feature store:
     device and SIM binding, account age, payee age, typical amounts,
     time of day, geo change, recent failed PIN attempts, graph links.
   Streaming jobs (Kafka) keep counters fresh in near real time.

4. Outcomes: ALLOW, CHALLENGE (extra confirmation, cooling period,
   warning for scam patterns), or BLOCK. Show clear messages.

5. Collect-request scams: warn when approving a "collect" request,
   since paying a stranger's request is a common fraud pattern.

6. Feedback loop: confirmed fraud and disputes retrain models; rules
   are tuned with shadow mode (log, don't act) before enforcing.

Cost of a false block is a frustrated user; cost of a missed
fraud is real money. Tune per amount and risk segment.
Fraud control belongs in the payment path, not after it. Keep the fast path cheap (rules plus precomputed features) and run heavier analysis asynchronously.

What we did NOT choose and why

Rejected alternatives

AlternativeWhy rejected
Assume timeout means failureCauses double charges or stranded money; use UNKNOWN plus enquiry.
Mutable balance with no historyLost updates, no audit trail, cannot reconcile.
Distributed 2PC across banksBanks cannot participate; use saga with reversal and reconciliation.
NoSQL with eventual consistency for moneyRisk of lost or duplicated updates; money needs ACID.
Fraud checks only in batchToo late to stop irreversible transfers.
Route PIN through our serversSecurity and compliance risk; the PIN goes only to the issuing bank.

What breaks at 10× scale and how we fix it

Problem — A bank or the switch slows down or fails

Timeouts rise, pending payments pile up, and retries could make it worse.

Fix

Per-bank circuit breakers and rate limits, retry with backoff and jitter, queued status enquiries, clear “bank is slow, try later” messaging, and never retry a debit without the same reference.

Problem — Festival traffic spike

Payment volume jumps 8× in minutes, especially at the same few merchants.

Fix

Autoscale stateless services, shard DBs ahead of known events, shed non-critical work (rewards, analytics) first, cache heavy reads, and batch credits to hot merchant accounts.

Problem — Duplicate callbacks and out-of-order events

The same success arrives twice, or a failure arrives after a success.

Fix

Conditional state transitions on a one-way state machine, idempotent handlers keyed by switch reference, and versioned events so stale ones are ignored.

Problem — Regional outage or database failover

A data center loss during in-flight payments could lose acknowledged writes.

Fix

Synchronous replication for money data inside a region, tested failover, active-active stateless tiers, recovery jobs that resume PENDING and UNKNOWN transactions, and reconciliation against bank files as the final safety net.

Cheat sheet

What to remember before walking into the room.

Key numbers

50M DAU · 100M txns/day · ~1.2K TPS avg, ~10K peak · < 3 s payment · < 100 ms fraud check

Key datastores

PostgreSQL / Spanner (transactions, ledger) · Redis (status, cache) · Feature store · Kafka · data lake

Payment strategy

Idempotency key, persist-then-call, UNKNOWN state, status enquiry, auto reversal, daily reconciliation.

Ledger strategy

Append-only double-entry, atomic conditional updates, saga for cross-shard, invariant monitoring.

Transaction state machine

INITIATED → PENDING → SUCCESS / FAILED, with UNKNOWN → resolved, and REVERSED after refunds.

Key tradeoffs

Correctness vs. latency. Block fraud vs. user friction. Strong consistency vs. availability under partner outages.

Hardest part

Knowing exactly what happened to money after a timeout from a system we do not control.
